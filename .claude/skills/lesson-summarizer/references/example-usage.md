## Example 1: Biology - Photosynthesis

### Input Lesson (simplified excerpt)
```
Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy from the sun into chemical energy that can be later released to fuel the plant's activities. This is done through a process called photosynthesis and requires three main ingredients: light energy, water, and carbon dioxide. The process happens primarily in the leaves of the plant...

[Full lesson continues with detailed explanation]
```

### What the Skill Delivers

#### Beginner-Friendly Summary

**Photosynthesis: How Plants Make Their Own Food**

Imagine a factory that turns sunlight into food. That's what plants do! Here's how:

Plants need three things: sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide from the air. Using these ingredients, they create glucose (a type of sugar that plants use as food) and release oxygen as a byproduct. This happens mainly in the leaves.

Think of it like this: Sunlight is like the power source, water and carbon dioxide are like raw materials, and the plant is like a factory. The "factory" (the plant's cells) uses light energy to combine these ingredients into glucose.

The process happens in two main stages:
1. **Light reactions** (in daylight): These use sunlight to split water molecules and create energy packets (ATP and NADPH) that power the next stage
2. **Dark reactions** (also called the Calvin Cycle): These use the energy from stage 1 to combine carbon dioxide into glucose

The cool part? Plants release oxygen as waste—which is what we breathe! So photosynthesis is literally how plants and animals support each other.

This process is why plants need sunlight, why they're green (the green pigment chlorophyll captures light), and why they grow—they're constantly making food and storing energy.

#### Exam-Ready Key Points

1. **Photosynthesis definition**: The process where plants convert sunlight into glucose (food) using water and carbon dioxide, releasing oxygen as a byproduct.

2. **Three ingredients needed**: Sunlight (energy), water (from roots), carbon dioxide (from air).

3. **Where it happens**: Primarily in the leaves, specifically in structures called chloroplasts.

4. **The two stages**: Light reactions (use sunlight, happen in daylight) and the Calvin Cycle/Dark reactions (make glucose, can happen anytime).

5. **Light reactions output**: Creates ATP and NADPH energy packets that power the next stage.

6. **Calvin Cycle function**: Uses energy from light reactions to combine CO2 into glucose.

7. **Chlorophyll role**: The green pigment that captures light energy; this is why plants are green.

8. **Byproduct**: Oxygen is released (which animals need to breathe).

9. **Why plants need sunlight**: To power the process of making glucose for growth and energy.

10. **Relationship with cellular respiration**: Plants make glucose through photosynthesis; cells use that glucose through respiration (the reverse process).

## Example 2: History - The French Revolution

### Input Lesson (simplified excerpt)
```
The French Revolution (1789-1799) was a period of significant social upheaval in France...
[Details about causes, events, key figures, consequences]
```

### What the Skill Delivers

#### Beginner-Friendly Summary

**The French Revolution: When France Turned Upside Down**

Imagine a country where the rich few had everything while millions of people starved. That was France in 1789. But the people decided things needed to change—dramatically.

**Why did people revolt?**
- The government was bankrupt (spent all its money)
- The poor paid high taxes while the rich paid almost nothing
- There wasn't enough food
- People wanted a say in government (like a democracy)

**What happened?**
The revolution wasn't a single event—it was a messy, violent period of about 10 years. Key moments included:
- July 14, 1789: Parisians stormed the Bastille (a prison, symbol of power)
- August 1789: Rights were declared (everyone should have freedoms)
- 1792: The king was arrested
- 1793: King Louis XVI was executed
- The "Reign of Terror" (1793-1794): Thousands of people were executed

**What changed?**
By the end, France had:
- A new type of government
- Laws written down (a Constitution)
- The idea that all people had rights (not just nobles)
- A new calendar and symbols (the tricolor flag)

**Why does it matter?**
The French Revolution inspired democratic movements worldwide. It showed that people could challenge unfair systems and create change, though the journey was violent and chaotic.

#### Exam-Ready Key Points

1. **Time period**: 1789-1799 (10 years of upheaval in France).

2. **Main causes**: Bankrupt government, unfair taxes (poor paid, rich didn't), food shortages, people wanted democracy.

3. **Three Estates**: Society was divided into clergy (1st), nobility (2nd), and commoners/peasants (3rd) who were treated unfairly.

4. **Storming the Bastille (July 14, 1789)**: Parisians attacked a prison symbol of royal power; marks the start of the revolution.

5. **Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (1789)**: Stated that all people have rights like liberty and equality.

6. **King Louis XVI**: The monarch who was eventually arrested and executed.

7. **Reign of Terror (1793-1794)**: Period where thousands were executed by guillotine for opposing the revolution.

8. **Key figures**: Robespierre (leader of the Terror), Marie Antoinette (queen, executed), Napoleon (eventually took power).

9. **Constitutional changes**: France moved from absolute monarchy to constitutional government.

10. **Lasting impact**: Inspired democratic movements worldwide; introduced the concepts of "liberty, equality, fraternity" and citizens' rights.

11. **Common misconception**: The revolution didn't immediately create a peaceful democracy—it was violent and chaotic for years.

## Example 3: Mathematics - Quadratic Equations

### Input Lesson (simplified excerpt)
```
A quadratic equation is a polynomial equation of the second degree...
[Includes explanation of standard form, methods to solve, applications]
```

### What the Skill Delivers

#### Beginner-Friendly Summary

**Quadratic Equations: Finding Unknown Numbers**

A quadratic equation is a math puzzle where you're finding a number (usually called x) and that number is squared (multiplied by itself). They look like this:

ax² + bx + c = 0

Don't let the letters scare you! Think of it like: (something squared) + (something) + (number) = 0

**Why do we care?**
These equations pop up everywhere: calculating ball flight in sports, building bridges, programming games. If you understand quadratics, you understand how the world works mathematically.

**Three ways to solve them:**

1. **Factoring** (easiest if possible): Break it into simpler multiplication pieces
   - Example: x² + 5x + 6 = 0 becomes (x+2)(x+3) = 0, so x = -2 or x = -3

2. **Completing the square** (middle difficulty): Rearrange to make a perfect square
   - Takes practice but always works

3. **Quadratic formula** (always works): Plug numbers into a formula
   - Formula: x = (-b ± √(b²-4ac)) / 2a
   - This is your safety net—if nothing else works, use this

**Key insight**: A quadratic equation can have 0, 1, or 2 solutions (answers).

#### Exam-Ready Key Points

1. **Standard form**: ax² + bx + c = 0 (where a ≠ 0).

2. **Quadratic formula**: x = (-b ± √(b²-4ac)) / 2a (always works; most important formula to memorize).

3. **Discriminant**: The part under the square root: b²-4ac. It tells you how many solutions exist.
   - If positive: 2 solutions
   - If zero: 1 solution
   - If negative: 0 real solutions

4. **Factoring method**: If ax²+bx+c can be factored, find factors that multiply to ac and add to b.

5. **Completing the square**: Rearrange the equation to create a perfect square trinomial.

6. **Vertex form**: y = a(x-h)² + k, where (h,k) is the vertex (highest/lowest point of the parabola).

7. **Roots/zeros**: The x-values where the quadratic equals zero (where it crosses the x-axis).

8. **Parabola**: The graph of a quadratic equation is always a U-shaped curve (opens up if a>0, down if a<0).

9. **Real vs. complex solutions**: If discriminant is negative, solutions are complex numbers (involve i), not real numbers.

10. **Applications**: Used in physics (projectile motion), business (profit/cost), engineering, and many other fields.

11. **Common mistake**: Not using ± in the quadratic formula—both plus and minus give different answers!

|---------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Science** | Process/mechanism, why it matters, real-world relevance | Definitions, steps, formulas, functions |
| **History** | Causes, key events, consequences, significance | Dates, figures, causes/effects, outcomes |
| **Math** | What it is, why we use it, how to solve it | Formulas, procedures, when to use each method |
| **Literature** | Plot, themes, character motivations | Characters, major events, themes, symbolism |
| **Language** | Rules, patterns, why they exist | Grammar rules, exceptions, example sentences |

## Tips from Real Usage

✓ **Start general then specific**: Don't assume knowledge of basic terms
✓ **Use student's language**: "Plants make food using sunlight" not "photoautotrophs utilize solar radiation"
✓ **One analogy per concept**: More confuses; less doesn't help
✓ **Diagram when complex**: Visual aids help with processes and structures
✓ **Test yourself**: Read the summary, THEN try to recall the exam points
✓ **Ask for adjustments**: "Make it simpler" or "Add more details on X" works great
